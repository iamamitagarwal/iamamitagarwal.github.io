**Abstract.** To assess how Large Language Models (LLMs) respond to accessibility-oriented educational queries, examining whether they produce helpful outputs (relevant, supportive, and task-answering) while avoiding harmful characteristics (stigmatizing language or accessibility conflicts), and how performance varies by disability context and model. We propose an evaluation framework in which an LLM judge performs token-level extraction from model responses and assigns a label (Relevance, Support, Answer, Stigma, Conflict). The study covers 16 LLMs answering 38 educator-facing questions spanning nine disability contexts. We compute validity via surface grounding (token present in the source), and aggregate distributions by metric label, disability type, question, and model. Across 122,684 extracted tokens, the distribution was dominated by helpful categories, with Support accounting for 65,794 tokens (53.7%) and Answer for 36,213 tokens (29.6%). Relevance constituted 19,458 tokens (15.9%). In contrast, harmful categories were comparatively rare, with only 285 Stigma tokens (0.2%) and 934 Conflict tokens (0.8%). These distributions were broadly consistent across the 16 evaluated LLMs, with only modest variation in the relative prevalence of each label. The framework yields a comprehensive view of LLM behavior on accessibility-focused educational questions. Current models can help surface practical accommodations and answer educators’ queries. However, despite the very low frequency of Stigma (0.2%) and Conflict (0.8%) tokens, model judgment on ethically sensitive content remains limited and unstable. We recommend human-in-the-loop use and targeted improvements to strengthen ethically sensitive dimensions.

**Summary.** This study assesses answers to accessibility-related teaching questions from 16 LLMs across nine disability contexts. A token-based judging framework analyzes relevance, support, answers, stigma and conflicts with accessibility needs. Helpful categories dominate the extracted tokens, while the authors caution that automated judgments of ethically sensitive content remain unreliable and recommend human oversight.
